# Presentation brief

Status: Approved

## Objective

- Topic: Whether Riverton should run a six-month bus-priority-lane pilot on a 6.2 km corridor.
- Desired change: Move the Riverton City Council from considering the proposal to a clear decision to authorize the six-month pilot with formal reviews at weeks 4, 12, and 24.
- Audience: Fictional Riverton city councillors making a public investment and street-allocation decision.
- Audience baseline: Decision-makers who need a concise account of the problem, expected effects, trade-offs, cost, uncertainty, and control points; no prior transit expertise is assumed.

## Delivery

- Duration: About 15 minutes.
- Planned slide count: Exactly 15 slides.
- Language: English.
- Venue and delivery format: Presenter-led city council decision presentation.
- Deliverables at this checkpoint: content-first Storyboard only; authored presentation, website, and any export follow approval.

## Content and evidence

- Must cover: the 6.2 km corridor; 18,400 weekday bus riders; the current 34-minute peak bus trip and plus-or-minus 9-minute variability; the finding that 58% of delay occurs at intersections; the expected 8-minute bus-trip reduction; the expected 3-minute increase in the typical car trip; unchanged emergency response; the $6.8 million cost; the six-month duration; and reviews at weeks 4, 12, and 24.
- Can omit: general transit education, comparisons with other cities, implementation details not supplied, benefit-cost calculations, ridership forecasts, traffic-volume claims, environmental claims, equity claims, construction schedules, funding sources, and operational thresholds.
- Claims requiring evidence: every visible number and every statement about expected travel-time, emergency-response, cost, timing, or review gates must trace only to the fictional evidence supplied by the user.
- Evidence boundary: no browsing and no additional measurements. Expected outcomes will be labeled as expectations, not observed results. The presentation will explicitly distinguish known inputs from questions the pilot would test.
- Assets: no external assets are required or permitted by the evidence brief. Simple authored diagrams and typographic data displays may represent only supplied facts.

## Direction

- Visible slide density: concise, presenter-led.
- Speaker-note strategy: visible slides carry one decision-relevant idea at a time; notes hold transitions, qualifications, evidence wording, and supporting detail needed for a roughly one-minute discussion per slide.
- Narrative approach: recommendation first, then decision criteria, current corridor burden, proposed effect, trade-offs, evidence limits, review architecture, and a final motion. Frame authorization as a controlled six-month test rather than a permanent policy conclusion.
- Visual direction: civic and evidence-led rather than campaign-like; use a corridor motif, large numeric anchors, clear expected-versus-current distinctions, and a restrained visual hierarchy suitable for a council chamber.
- Motion intensity: restrained. Reveals may pace comparisons and the review sequence, but motion must not imply unsupported causality or precision.
- Interaction intent: none; the deck should work as a linear 15-minute council presentation.
- Visual, motion, or interaction references: none supplied; derive a topic-specific municipal decision system after Storyboard approval.

## Visual foundation

- Canvas: preserve the configured 1600 × 900 canvas in `drever.config.ts` exactly.
- Safe area and default content inset: keep all meaningful content inside a 72 px safe area on every edge, with a default 96 px content inset; persistent accents may enter the outer safe area but may not carry essential content.
- Surface owner: a custom project-local Theme will own typography, color, and component styling; a matching Stage will own the full background; one persistent corridor-line accent may be owned by the Theme/Stage pair. No starter rail or competing background will remain.
- Last-known-good layout checkpoint: Draft 1 will establish the stable grid, panel padding, and readable line wraps after approval; later visual and motion work must preserve that baseline.

## Slide outline

1. **A controlled pilot, with three chances to review** — Opening; state the recommended council action and establish that the decision is about a six-month test with reviews at weeks 4, 12, and 24. Evidence: six-month pilot and supplied review gates. Takeaway: the council can make a clear, bounded pilot decision tonight.
2. **The decision standard** — Context; define the four questions the council must weigh: rider need, expected travel-time effect, effects on other road users and emergency response, and cost with oversight. Evidence: categories derived solely from the supplied decision inputs. Takeaway: the presentation will judge the pilot against explicit decision criteria.
3. **One corridor, 18,400 weekday riders** — Evidence; establish the scale of the corridor and the population directly exposed to current bus performance. Evidence: 6.2 km and 18,400 weekday bus riders. Takeaway: this is a concentrated, high-ridership corridor decision.
4. **The peak bus trip is 34 minutes—and unpredictable** — Evidence; show the current trip duration and variability without converting them into unsupported percentiles or ranges of observed performance. Evidence: 34 minutes with plus or minus 9 minutes variability. Takeaway: the status quo problem is both time and uncertainty.
5. **Most delay occurs at intersections** — Claim; identify where the supplied evidence locates the dominant delay burden while avoiding unsupported engineering prescriptions. Evidence: 58% of delay occurs at intersections. Takeaway: the delay problem is concentrated, not diffuse.
6. **The expected bus trip: 34 minutes to 26** — Comparison; make the proposed 8-minute improvement concrete and label 26 minutes as an expected result, not an observed one. Evidence: current 34 minutes and expected 8-minute reduction. Takeaway: the pilot offers a material expected gain for bus travel time.
7. **The trade-off: three minutes for the typical car trip** — Comparison; put the expected car impact beside the expected bus gain without claiming a full network effect. Evidence: expected 3-minute addition to the typical car trip and expected 8-minute bus reduction. Takeaway: the proposal involves an explicit travel-time trade-off.
8. **Emergency response is expected to remain unchanged** — Claim; isolate the emergency-response constraint and keep the wording faithful to the supplied expectation. Evidence: emergency response unchanged. Takeaway: the proposal is not expected to change emergency response.
9. **The six-month test costs $6.8 million** — Evidence; state the full supplied cost and connect it to the pilot decision without inventing unit costs, funding sources, or payback. Evidence: $6.8 million and six-month duration. Takeaway: authorization carries a clear and material fiscal commitment.
10. **The case, on one page** — Comparison; synthesize the supplied case for and against: 18,400 weekday riders, an expected 8-minute bus gain, an expected 3-minute typical car penalty, unchanged emergency response, and $6.8 million cost. Evidence: only the supplied figures. Takeaway: the council can see the whole decision balance without hidden assumptions.
11. **What the forecast does not decide** — Explanation; distinguish expected results from measured pilot outcomes and identify that actual bus time, car time, emergency response, and cost performance remain to be reviewed. Evidence: supplied items are expectations or proposal inputs; no additional measurements are available. Takeaway: uncertainty is a reason for disciplined review, not for presenting forecasts as facts.
12. **Six months turns a forecast into a decision record** — Process; explain the role of a time-bounded pilot in testing the supplied expectations over 24 weeks. Evidence: six-month duration and week-24 review. Takeaway: the pilot is structured to produce a later decision grounded in observed performance.
13. **Three review gates keep the council in control** — Process; sequence the reviews at weeks 4, 12, and 24 and give each its decision role without inventing numerical pass/fail thresholds. Evidence: supplied review weeks. Takeaway: oversight is built into the pilot calendar.
14. **The choice: test the proposal or retain the status quo** — Decision; place the recommended authorization against the alternative of not running the pilot, using only the established evidence and uncertainties. Evidence: full supplied decision record. Takeaway: the evidence supports a controlled test while acknowledging the stated cost and road-user trade-off.
15. **Authorize the six-month pilot** — Close; present a direct motion to approve the $6.8 million pilot with reviews at weeks 4, 12, and 24. Evidence: cost, duration, and review gates. Takeaway: vote yes on the pilot and require the three scheduled reviews.

## Assumptions and open risks

- The recommendation is to authorize the pilot, because the user requested a decision presentation and delegated remaining direction choices.
- “Plus or minus 9 minutes variability” will be quoted exactly; the deck will not infer a statistical definition or calculate unsupported endpoints.
- The expected 26-minute bus trip is the arithmetic result of the supplied 34-minute current trip minus the supplied 8-minute expected reduction; it will be labeled expected.
- No claim will be made that the pilot reduces variability, changes ridership, produces environmental or equity benefits, or resolves all intersection delay.
- No numerical success thresholds, stop conditions, implementation methods, or review metrics were supplied. The three gates will therefore be framed as council review points, not as invented automatic pass/fail tests.
- The stated unchanged emergency response will be presented as a proposal expectation consistent with the supplied evidence, not as an already observed pilot outcome.
