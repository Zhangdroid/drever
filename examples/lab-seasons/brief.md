# Presentation brief

Status: Approved

## Objective

- Topic: Why seasons are not caused by Earth moving closer to the Sun.
- Desired change: Students can reject the distance misconception and explain, in their own words, how Earth's axial tilt changes Sun angle and daylight length to produce opposite seasons in the two hemispheres.
- Audience: Students aged 14 to 16.
- Audience baseline: Familiar with Earth orbiting the Sun and with the names of the seasons; may hold or recognize the common closer-means-summer misconception.

## Delivery

- Duration: About 12 minutes.
- Planned slide count: 15 slides.
- Language: English.
- Venue and delivery format: Teacher- or student-presented classroom talk on a projected screen.
- Deliverables: Live Drever presentation and website; no PDF requested at this stage.

## Content and evidence

- Must cover: The distance hypothesis and what it predicts; Earth being closest to the Sun in early January; opposite seasons in the hemispheres; Earth's axial tilt of about 23.4 degrees; the effects of a higher Sun angle and longer daylight; a causal explanation students can retell.
- Can omit: Detailed orbital mechanics, eccentricity calculations, climate-zone exceptions, ocean-atmosphere lag, solstices and equinoxes as calendar detail, and the historical discovery of axial tilt.
- Claims or decisions that require evidence: Use only the supplied facts as factual anchors. Make the explanation of Sun angle spreading the same incoming energy over a different area physically clear without adding numerical claims. Avoid implying that tilt makes a hemisphere meaningfully closer to the Sun.
- Preferred source authority: User-supplied facts; no browsing or external research.
- Provided or permitted assets: No external assets supplied. Later visuals should be original schematic diagrams built in the deck, with labels rather than photographic imagery.

## Direction

- Visible slide density: Concise, presenter-led slides with supporting explanation in speaker notes.
- Speaker-note strategy: Notes carry the spoken reasoning, anticipated misconception checks, brief transitions, and a model explanation; visible slides keep to one idea and minimal labels.
- Narrative approach: Prediction → contradiction → mechanism → teach-back. Treat “closer to the Sun” as a testable idea rather than ridiculing it, use two decisive contradictions, then assemble the real causal chain.
- Visual direction: A clean classroom-science system inspired by an annotated astronomy notebook: deep night-blue field, warm sunlight, cool Earth tones, bold geometric diagrams, and very limited decorative detail. Prioritize legibility and causal arrows over realism.
- Motion intensity: Restrained. Use progressive reveals only when they help students predict an answer or assemble the causal chain.
- Interaction intent: Lightweight whole-class participation through two quick prediction prompts and a final explain-to-a-partner challenge; no custom interactive component is required.
- Visual, motion, or interaction references: No external reference requested. Keep all diagrams schematic, consistent, and easy to redraw from memory.

## Slide outline

1. **Why summer is not “closer”** — Opening. Surface the familiar explanation and frame the talk as a mystery students can solve. Evidence: the distance claim as a testable hypothesis. Anchor: one provocative question.
2. **Make the idea predict** — Context. Ask what should happen worldwide if distance caused seasons. Evidence: a shared Earth–Sun distance should imply the same seasonal response. Anchor: the hypothesis-to-prediction statement.
3. **Clue 1: January** — Evidence. Show that Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, when it is winter in the Northern Hemisphere. Evidence: supplied perihelion timing. Anchor: “Closest: early January.”
4. **Clue 2: opposite seasons** — Evidence. Establish that the hemispheres experience opposite seasons at the same time. Evidence: supplied hemispheric-season fact. Anchor: a paired North/South seasonal comparison.
5. **The distance idea fails both tests** — Claim. Combine the two contradictions and retire distance as the main mechanism. Evidence: January timing plus opposite hemispheres. Anchor: a two-check verdict.
6. **Meet the real cause: a tilted Earth** — Explanation. Introduce the approximately 23.4° axial tilt as the key geometry. Evidence: supplied tilt value. Anchor: the tilted rotation axis.
7. **The tilt keeps its direction** — Explanation. Clarify that during the orbit, one hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun while the other is tilted away. Evidence: required schematic reasoning, with no added numerical claim. Anchor: the same-axis-direction idea.
8. **Tilt changes two things** — Section. Preview the two effects that matter: Sun angle and daylight duration. Evidence: supplied higher-angle and longer-daylight facts. Anchor: the two-branch causal chain.
9. **Effect 1: a higher Sun angle** — Explanation. Explain that more direct sunlight concentrates incoming energy over a smaller surface area. Evidence: geometric comparison without numerical claims. Anchor: equal light beams across unequal ground areas.
10. **Effect 2: longer daylight** — Explanation. Explain that more hours of daylight provide more time for solar energy to arrive. Evidence: supplied longer-daylight fact. Anchor: short-day versus long-day timelines.
11. **Put the summer chain together** — Process. Assemble tilt toward → higher Sun angle + longer daylight → more heating → summer. Evidence: the mechanism established on slides 6–10. Anchor: one causal chain.
12. **The other hemisphere gets the reverse** — Comparison. Apply the same mechanism simultaneously to the opposite hemisphere. Evidence: opposite seasons plus the same two effects reversing. Anchor: mirrored hemispheric chains.
13. **What distance can’t explain—but tilt can** — Comparison. Contrast the predictions of the distance idea with the observations explained by tilt. Evidence: early-January closest approach and opposite seasons. Anchor: a concise two-model test.
14. **Can you explain it in four moves?** — Demo. Give students a reusable explanation structure: reject distance, name tilt, describe the two effects, connect them to opposite seasons. Evidence: synthesis of the full argument. Anchor: four numbered verbal moves.
15. **Your turn: teach it back** — Close. Prompt a partner explanation and end with the memorable causal sentence. Evidence: the approved mechanism only. Anchor: “Tilt changes angle and daylight—not Earth’s seasonal distance.”

## Assumptions and open risks

- The talk is presenter-led, so notes may be fuller than visible copy while remaining paced for 12 minutes.
- “Early January” is intentionally used instead of a precise date because that is the supplied evidence.
- The diagrams must avoid exaggerated Earth–Sun distance changes or an exaggerated tilt that could reinforce the misconception.
- Students may confuse “tilted toward” with “much closer”; the spoken explanation and diagrams should explicitly separate orientation from orbital distance.
- The plan uses no researched claims, external assets, or citations because browsing is prohibited and the supplied facts are sufficient for the learning goal.
