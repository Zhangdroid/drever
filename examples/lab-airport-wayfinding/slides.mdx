  <p className="thesis">Design for the glance. Reward the read.</p>

<Note>
  Open inside the experience, not with a definition. You are moving, your gate may be far away, and
  you are already filtering announcements, people, bags, and time. Yet a good sign can still produce
  a confident next step in a second or two. This talk is not about copying the visual style of
  airports. It is about extracting the operating principles behind good wayfinding and applying them
  to product moments where attention is scarce. The promise is practical: six principles and one
  short pre-ship test.
</Note>

  <p className="bottom-line">Attention is already spent before the interface appears.</p>
</div>

<Note>
  The useful design move is to model the state in which the interface is encountered. In an airport,
  people are often walking, looking for landmarks, listening for changes, and holding a destination
  in working memory. Product interfaces create similar conditions during checkout recovery, incident
  response, permissions, travel, onboarding, or any interruption-heavy workflow. “Rushed” here does
  not only mean a clock is visible. It means the user has less capacity to read, compare, remember,
  and recover than the team designing the screen.
</Note>

  <h2>Rushed is not the same as simple.</h2>
  <div className="needs-cycle">
    <div className="need need-top">
      <span>◎</span>
      <strong>Orient</strong>
      <small>Where am I?</small>
    </div>
    <div className="need need-right">
      <span>↗</span>
      <strong>Choose</strong>
      <small>Which way now?</small>
    </div>
    <div className="need need-bottom">
      <span>●</span>
      <strong>Act</strong>
      <small>What do I do?</small>
    </div>
    <div className="need need-left">
      <span>✓</span>
      <strong>Confirm</strong>
      <small>Did it work?</small>
    </div>
    <div className="rushed-core">RUSHED</div>
  </div>
  <p className="bottom-line">Less copy cannot replace a clear sequence.</p>
</div>

<Note>
  Teams often respond to low attention by removing information. Sometimes that helps, but the
  underlying task can still be complex. A rushed user still has to orient, choose, act, and confirm.
  If we remove the landmarks, sequence, or reassurance that support those jobs, the interface
  becomes sparse but not clear. Airport signs work because they structure complexity into moments.
  The question is not “How little can we show?” It is “What does this person need to know at this
  exact point in the journey?”
</Note>

<div className="slide draft principle-slide">
  <p className="principle-number">01</p>
  <h2>
    Announce destination
    <br />
    before detail.
  </h2>
  <div className="comparison-strip">
    <div className="weak">
      <small>SYSTEM FIRST</small>
      <p>Complete identity verification to continue</p>
    </div>
    <div className="strong">
      <small>DESTINATION FIRST</small>
      <p>
        <span>Continue to your gate</span> <b>→</b>
      </p>
      <em>Identity check ahead</em>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<Note>
  Good wayfinding begins with the destination the traveler recognizes, not the system procedure
  behind it. Product interfaces often reverse this: they lead with an internal requirement, error
  code, or policy and make the user infer the goal. Start with where the user can go or what they
  can accomplish. Then explain the condition. The interface example is deliberately hypothetical:
  “Continue to your gate” gives the goal first; “Identity check ahead” supplies the necessary detail
  without making the procedure the headline.
</Note>

<div className="slide draft">
  <p className="eyebrow">INFORMATION HAS A HORIZON</p>
  <h2>Hierarchy changes with distance.</h2>
  <div className="horizon-track">
    <div>
      <strong>DECIDE</strong>
      <span>from afar</span>
      <p>Gates A–D →</p>
    </div>
    <i>→</i>
    <div>
      <strong>CONFIRM</strong>
      <span>at the choice</span>
      <p>Gate C →</p>
    </div>
    <i>→</i>
    <div>
      <strong>READ</strong>
      <span>when stopped</span>
      <p>Boarding details</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <p className="bottom-line">Design for the attention window, not the screen size.</p>
</div>

<Note>
  The same journey needs different information at different distances. From afar, people need a
  large category or destination. At the junction, they need an unmistakable directional
  confirmation. Once stopped, they can read detailed instructions. Interfaces have equivalent
  horizons: a notification, a landing state, a decision point, and a detail view. Responsive design
  usually means adapting to device dimensions. This adds another question: what can the user
  perceive in the time available before they must decide?
</Note>

<div className="slide draft principle-slide">
  <p className="principle-number">02</p>
  <h2>
    One decision
    <br />
    at each decision point.
  </h2>
  <div className="fork-map">
    <div className="you-are-here">YOU ARE HERE</div>
    <MotionGroup name="decision-token" intent="continuity">
      <span className="decision-token">NOW</span>
    </MotionGroup>
    <div className="current-fork">
      <span>← Gates A–D</span>
      <span>Gates E–H →</span>
    </div>
    <div className="future-forks">
      <span>Later: lounges</span>
      <span>Later: connections</span>
      <span>Later: baggage</span>
    </div>
  </div>
  <p className="bottom-line">Future choices are not helpful at the current fork.</p>
</div>

<Note>
  At a junction, a useful sign answers the choice available now. It does not ask you to plan every
  later turn. Product flows often expose configuration, exceptions, and future decisions too early
  because the system knows they will eventually matter. That creates a wall of choices at the moment
  of least context. Identify the actual fork. Show the alternatives that can be acted on now. Defer
  later branches until the user reaches the point where those choices become meaningful.
</Note>

<div className="slide draft principle-slide">
  <p className="principle-number">03</p>
  <h2>
    Reveal detail
    <br />
    in sequence.
  </h2>
  <div className="sequence-chain">
    <div>
      <MotionGroup name="decision-token" intent="continuity">
        <span className="decision-token">NOW</span>
      </MotionGroup>
      <strong>Destination</strong>
      <small>Can I get there?</small>
    </div>
    <div>
      <span>2</span>
      <strong>Direction</strong>
      <small>Which way now?</small>
    </div>
    <div>
      <span>3</span>
      <strong>Confirmation</strong>
      <small>Am I still right?</small>
    </div>
    <div>
      <span>4</span>
      <strong>Local detail</strong>
      <small>What happens here?</small>
    </div>
  </div>
  <p className="bottom-line">Every reveal should answer the next question.</p>
</div>

<Note>
  Progressive disclosure is often treated as a visual technique: hide details in a drawer,
  accordion, or secondary screen. Wayfinding suggests a stronger model. Disclosure is a route
  through questions. First: can I reach the destination? Then: which direction? Then: am I still on
  course? Finally: what do I need to know here? A good product sequence is not merely less content
  per screen. Each stage deliberately resolves one question and creates the context needed for the
  next.
</Note>

<div className="slide draft principle-slide">
  <p className="principle-number">04</p>
  <h2>Consistency beats cleverness.</h2>
  <div className="vocabulary">
    <div className="key">
      <span className="shape square">A</span>
      <strong>Destination</strong>
    </div>
    <div className="route-repeat">
      <span className="shape square">A</span>
      <b>→</b>
      <span className="shape square">A</span>
      <b>→</b>
      <span className="shape square">A</span>
    </div>
    <p>Same label. Same shape. Same meaning.</p>
  </div>
  <p className="bottom-line">Recognition is faster than reinterpretation.</p>
</div>

<Note>
  Airport navigation becomes learnable because a small vocabulary repeats. A category retains its
  name, symbols behave consistently, and directional cues keep their meaning. In product work, local
  optimization can make every screen individually “better” while making the journey harder to learn.
  A clever label at one step, a new icon at the next, and a different status color after that all
  charge an interpretation cost. Under pressure, consistency is not boring. It is cached
  understanding.
</Note>

<div className="slide draft principle-slide">
  <p className="principle-number">05</p>
  <h2>Use redundant cues.</h2>
  <div className="redundancy-core">
    <div className="instruction">
      <span className="gate-chip">C12</span>
      <strong>Gate C12</strong>
      <b>→</b>
    </div>
    <div className="cue-list">
      <span>WORD</span>
      <span>SHAPE</span>
      <span>POSITION</span>
      <span>DIRECTION</span>
    </div>
  </div>
  <p className="bottom-line">The message should survive the loss of one cue.</p>
</div>

<Note>
  Redundancy is sometimes mistaken for visual noise. Useful redundancy lets multiple independent
  cues reinforce the same instruction. A traveler may miss the color, not recognize the icon, have
  limited vision, or approach from an unexpected angle. The words, shape, position, and direction
  can still agree. Product teams should ask whether a critical status or action depends on color
  alone, an unfamiliar symbol, a transient toast, or a subtle position change. Important meaning
  deserves more than one path to comprehension.
</Note>

        <strong>Gate C · 4 min</strong>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
  <p className="bottom-line">Silence after action creates doubt.</p>
</div>

<Note>
  Navigation does not end when someone chooses a direction. Between junctions, small confirmations
  preserve confidence: the route continues, the destination remains named, and progress is visible.
  Product interfaces often celebrate the action but then remove the context. A button disappears,
  the next screen has a different label, or a background operation offers no durable state.
  Confirmation should answer three quiet questions: did the action register, am I still on the
  intended route, and what should I expect next?
</Note>

<div className="slide draft principle-slide">
  <p className="principle-number">06</p>
  <h2>
    Separate scan mode
    <br />
    from read mode.
  </h2>
  <div className="mode-pair">
    <div className="scan-mode">
      <small>SCAN MODE · MOVING</small>
      <strong>Gate changed</strong>
      <p>C12 → C18</p>
    </div>
    <div className="read-mode">
      <small>READ MODE · PAUSED</small>
      <strong>Why it changed</strong>
      <p>Details, assistance, and alternatives remain available when the user can stop.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <p className="bottom-line">Urgent users should not have to parse prose.</p>
</div>

<Note>
  A sign designed for walking and a directory designed for standing can coexist because they serve
  different attention modes. Products should make the urgent action and current state glanceable,
  while keeping explanation available in a place where reading is safe. Do not force the short
  message to carry every caveat, and do not force the detailed message to function as the alert.
  This is not about hiding information. It is about giving information the right moment.
</Note>

  <h2>Translate the sign into the interface.</h2>
  <div className="translation-grid">

      <strong>Product response</strong>
    </div>

          <span>Lead recovery with the user’s goal</span>
        </div>

          <span>Ask only the decision available now</span>
        </div>
        <div>
          <span>Repeat route cues</span>
          <span>Preserve labels, status, and progress</span>
        </div>
        <div>
          <span>Separate sign from directory</span>
          <span>Separate urgent action from explanation</span>
        </div>
      </div>
    </Step>
  </div>
</div>

<Note>
  The transfer is behavioral, not cosmetic. A checkout recovery screen does not need to look like a
  terminal sign. It can still lead with the outcome, isolate the current choice, preserve labels
  across steps, and confirm progress. The same applies to incident response, onboarding, and
  permission prompts. If we copy yellow arrows and condensed type, we have borrowed a style. If we
  structure attention and decisions, we have borrowed the useful part.
</Note>

  <h2>Start with the decision map.</h2>
  <div className="method-route">
    <div>
      <span>01</span>
      <strong>Identify the goal</strong>
    </div>
    <b>→</b>
    <div>
      <span>02</span>
      <strong>Mark decision points</strong>
    </div>
    <b>→</b>
    <div>

    </div>
    <b>→</b>
    <div>
      <div className="method-marker">
        <span className="method-checkpoint" />
        <span className="method-marker__label">04</span>
      </div>
      <strong>Assign cue + confirmation</strong>
    </div>
  </div>
  <p className="bottom-line">Structure the journey before styling the screens.</p>
</div>

<Note>
  Here is the method teams can use immediately. First, name the destination in the user’s language.
  Second, mark every place where a choice or recovery is required. Third, describe the likely
  attention window at that point: moving or paused, first-time or familiar, calm or disrupted.
  Fourth, assign the cue that enables the choice and the confirmation that preserves orientation
  afterward. Only then design the screens. This often reveals that the problem is not a component
  problem; it is a sequencing problem.
</Note>

<Note>
  Use this as a fast design review. Do not ask the team to admire the screen. Ask them to point to
  the destination, the current decision, the next question, the stable cues, the redundant meaning,
  and the boundary between scanning and reading. If those answers require a walkthrough from the
  designer, the rushed user will not have them. This is not a substitute for usability work. It is a
  compact way to catch structural ambiguity before shipping or testing.
</Note>

  further. Announce destination before detail. Put one decision at each decision point. Reveal
  detail in sequence. Prefer consistency over cleverness. Use redundant cues. Separate scan mode
  from read mode. The result is not merely fewer words. It is confident movement through complexity.
</Note>
