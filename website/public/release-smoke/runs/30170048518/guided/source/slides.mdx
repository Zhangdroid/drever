Set the tone: calm, specific, and blameless. The fictional event is only the failure frame; do not invent a fictional product or fill the room with hypothetical details.

<Note>
Read the frame once, then give the room silence. Ask each person for three short failure statements. Silent writing reduces anchoring by the most senior or fastest speaker.

Advance once to reveal the sentence structure if the notes are vague. “Because a dependency had no rollback path, recovery stalled, visible as an unresolved rehearsal failure” is useful structure; keep any real content grounded in this launch.

Do not organize by product, design, and engineering. That turns risks into territorial handoffs. Prompt across value, reach, readiness, and trust, then cluster by the failure mechanism the notes share.
</Note>

  <p className="map-caption">Position by evidence—not volume, certainty, or job title.</p>

<Note>
Ask the group to cluster notes that describe the same failure mechanism. Read each cluster aloud in neutral language, then place it quickly on two axes: launch impact and evidence of plausibility.

“Evidence” can be a dependency, prior incident, unresolved test, customer signal, or missing capability. The map is directional, not a scoring model. Spend seconds, not minutes, on exact placement.

Once the clusters settle, advance once to lock the upper-right sector and shift the room from mapping to decision. High-impact items with stronger evidence become candidates for ownership. High-impact items with weak evidence may deserve a tripwire rather than immediate mitigation. This prevents a dramatic but ungrounded risk from crowding out a plausible one.
</Note>

      </Step>
      <Step>

          <span>Impact first, then harder to detect, then stronger evidence.</span>
        </div>
      </Step>
      <Step>
        <div className="commit-step">
          <strong>Commit</strong>
          <span>Stop at three. Name the rest as watch items.</span>
        </div>
      </Step>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<Note>
Reveal the decision sequence one action at a time. First, each person votes privately with three dots and may place only one dot on a risk. Private voting prevents the first argument from setting the ranking.

If the boundary around third place is tied, use the same order every time: greater impact, then harder-to-detect failure, then stronger evidence of plausibility. Do not split the difference by adding a fourth owner. The constraint is the value: it forces a portfolio decision the group can actually staff.

Check the three gates before committing. If no action or observable tripwire can be named, rewrite the risk until it is operational—or move it to the watch list.
</Note>

<Note>
For each of the three risks, capture four things: the sharp risk statement, the earliest observable signal, one accountable person, and the next move with a date. A team is not an owner. The owner coordinates; they do not have to do all the work.

If mitigation is premature, record a tripwire and the decision it will trigger: “If signal X appears by date Y, we do Z.” Confirm each owner aloud before closing.

End by repeating the reusable loop: frame failure, write independently, map together, choose three, assign one person to each. Schedule the next scan when the launch meaningfully changes—not merely because the calendar moved.
</Note>
