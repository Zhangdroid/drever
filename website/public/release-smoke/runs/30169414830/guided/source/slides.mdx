Explain that a readiness review asks whether the plan is complete; a pre-mortem asks which failure mechanisms the plan is underestimating. The purpose is not pessimism or exhaustive risk logging. The purpose is to surface weak signals early enough to choose three launch controls.

<Note>
**Timing — 1:10**

This is the whole working session. Name the two modes: the first seven minutes protect independent judgment; the remaining eighteen build shared judgment and commitment.

Facilitator setup before the meeting: bring the launch goal, date, non-negotiable constraints, and a simple place to write one failure per note. Invite only people who can explain the product outcome, customer experience, and technical operation. Five to seven people is ideal; a larger room should split into smaller groups.

On the reveal, emphasize the mode change. Discussion is deliberately delayed. This prevents the most senior or fastest speaker from defining the risk field before others have contributed.
</Note>

<Note>
**Timing — 1:15**

Read the prompt exactly. “Failed” must connect to the launch outcome: adoption, trust, reliability, revenue, or operational load. Ask for observable failure stories, not vague categories such as “technical risk.”

Give everyone five silent minutes. One failure per note. Encourage product, design, and engineering leads to write outside their own function. Do not debate, defend the plan, or propose mitigations yet.

If the room stalls, use only one neutral probe at a time: “What would customers experience?”, “What would we see too late?”, or “Where are we relying on an untested assumption?” Do not seed the room with your favorite risk.

On the reveal, state the rules. Silence is the mechanism that preserves independent evidence; it is not merely a time-saving tactic.
</Note>

<Note>
**Timing — 1:30**

Cluster duplicate stories by causal mechanism. “Onboarding,” “engineering,” or “operations” are buckets, not causes. Ask: “Would the same intervention reduce these failures?” If yes, they belong together. If no, split them.

For each serious cluster, write one sentence: “Because [mechanism], [failure] may cause [material impact]. Earliest signal: [observable evidence].” The signal can be a rehearsal result, missing instrumentation, an operational threshold, or a customer behavior—not a feeling.

Use the example to show the difference. The original story is vivid but hard to compare. The risk track exposes a testable mechanism and a signal that can change a launch decision.

Do not solve yet. Clarifying before mitigating keeps an attractive solution from making a weakly defined risk seem important.
</Note>

<Note>
**Timing — 1:50**

This is the decision point. First, each person privately marks the three risks they believe most deserve launch attention. Private marking keeps the first public argument from anchoring the vote.

Bring the five highest-marked risks into a six-minute challenge. For each, ask three questions:

1. If this happens, is the harm material to the launch outcome or customer trust?
2. Is there a credible mechanism or evidence—not merely an imaginable scenario?
3. Can action before launch reduce the odds, reduce the harm, or improve detection?

Do not multiply numeric scores; the precision is usually fictional. Surface disagreements instead. A split vote often exposes different evidence or assumptions.

Then force-rank. If the group cannot choose, compare risks pairwise: “If we could only control one before launch, which would we regret ignoring?” Stop at three. The rejected risks are not erased; they can remain on a watch list, but they do not receive the meeting’s scarce ownership attention.

The named examples are illustrative, not universal answers.
</Note>

<Note>
**Timing — 1:30**

Close by converting each selected risk into one owner card. The owner is a single accountable person, not a function. They may coordinate a team, but the room should know who returns with evidence.

Require five fields: the risk, the before-launch action, the earliest signal, the trigger that changes behavior, and the checkpoint when the room will review it. A mitigation without a trigger often becomes a task that never affects the launch decision.

If no useful action exists before launch, define monitoring and contingency. If neither is possible, ask whether the team is consciously accepting an unbounded risk. That may be valid, but it must be explicit.

End with: “A useful pre-mortem does not predict every failure. It buys focus while the team can still act.” Then ask the leads to book the 25-minute session before go/no-go and bring the three completed owner cards into that meeting.
</Note>
