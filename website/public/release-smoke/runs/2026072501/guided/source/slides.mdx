<Note>
Open with the outcome, not the ritual: “In eight minutes, you’ll have a method for deciding which three launch risks need names next to them.”

A pre-mortem is not pessimism and it is not prediction. It is a temporary change of tense that lets people say what they may hesitate to say in a normal readiness review.

Tell the group that the example is reusable across product, design, and engineering. The goal is not a complete risk register; it is a short list that changes launch behavior.

<Note>
At Step 0, let the “+30 days” frame settle before anyone starts solving. Advance once, then read the revealed prompt exactly and give the room a beat of silence.

Why “30 days later”? It is far enough away for consequences to be visible but close enough that people can picture the launch mechanics. Adjust the horizon for the product, but keep the failure concrete.

The three rules matter:

- Past tense makes the imagined failure feel discussable.
- No defending keeps seniority and functional identity from suppressing weak signals.
- No solving yet prevents the first plausible idea from swallowing the room.

If someone says, “That would never happen,” respond: “Great—capture why. Evidence comes in the selection round.”
</Note>

<Note>
At Step 0, establish “silent first” and point to the five-minute timer. Advance once to fan out the three scanning lenses.

Give everyone five quiet minutes. Silent generation reduces anchoring and gives design, product, and engineering equal airtime before hierarchy enters.

Ask for one failure per note. Quantity first: target three to five notes per person.

Use the lanes as prompts, not ownership boundaries. A designer may spot an operational risk; an engineer may spot a trust failure. Cross-functional duplication is useful evidence.

After five minutes, cluster duplicates quickly without wordsmithing. Do not ask authors to present every note. Read cluster labels aloud and allow one clarifying sentence only when the meaning is unclear.
</Note>

<Note>
At Step 0, build the grammar from left to right: cause, failure, impact. Advance once to resolve the template into a concrete risk slip.

Vague risks cannot be owned. Convert each serious cluster into a causal statement:

“Because [cause], [failure] may happen, causing [material impact].”

The example spans all three disciplines: permission design, admin workflow, and activation outcome. That is intentional. The statement is specific enough to test without prematurely prescribing a fix.

Watch for three failure modes:

- “Users might be confused” has no cause or consequence.
- “We need better onboarding” is already a solution.
- “The launch could fail” is circular.

Spend roughly a minute tightening only the clusters that could plausibly become top risks. Leave the rest rough.
</Note>

<Note>
Do not average a complicated score. Discuss each cluster through three filters:

1. Would the impact materially change launch success, trust, revenue, support load, or recovery?
2. Is there evidence that makes the risk plausible: research, incidents, unknown dependencies, load tests, or repeated disagreement?
3. Would the team detect it early enough to recover? Weak or late signals raise ownership priority.

At Step 0, hold on the filters. Ask: “Which risks are both consequential and easy to discover too late?”

Advance once to reveal three example owner slots. The examples are intentionally cross-functional. Replace them with the team’s actual risks in a live session.

Use dot votes only to surface preference, not to decide automatically. Discuss the top five and make an explicit call. If the team cannot stop at three, ask which fourth risk would displace one already selected. That forces a real priority decision.
</Note>

<Note>
Close with the complete facilitation plan.

For each selected risk, capture:

- One directly responsible owner—not a function or committee.
- One trigger or leading indicator that can be checked before launch.
- One next mitigation or information-gathering action with a date.

Suggested close: “The pre-mortem is useful only if it changes who watches what before launch. Book 25 minutes, stop at three, and put names next to them.”

If time is tight in the live session, protect silent writing and the final ownership step. Shorten clustering; do not turn the exercise into an unprioritized brainstorm.
</Note>
